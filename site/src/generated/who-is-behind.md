# Taxpayers fund the arenas, owners keep the upside

NBA arena deals are driven by team owners whose wealth increasingly comes from finance, backed by passive institutional investors and carried by a small, repeat cast of lobbyists, chambers of commerce, consultants and banks, and the deals they produce give the public no contractual share of the rise in team values that public money helps support. As of October 18, 2026, **11 of the 30 controlling owners made their fortunes in private equity, hedge funds, mortgage lending, asset management or venture capital, and four institutional investors (Arctos, owned by KKR since May 2026; Blue Owl HomeCourt, whose fees the NBA itself shares; Sixth Street; and the Qatar Investment Authority) hold stakes, mostly 5% to 20%, in 11 teams**, so 16 teams have one or the other (a, from the sources in Tables 1 and 2); a reported Arctos stake in the Grizzlies, seen only in search results, would make it 12 and 17. The funds are passive by league rule and no record places them at a negotiating table, but their returns come from rising team values, and Washington shows how the timing can work: the Qatar fund bought about 5% of the Wizards' parent at a $4.05 billion valuation in June 2023 ([AP via WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/sports/2023/06/22/qatar-investment-authority-is-buying-a-stake-in-monumental-sports-entertainment-ap-source-says/), c), the owner announced a move to Virginia that December ([USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/2023/12/12/washington-wizards-capitals-virginia-relocation-possible/71900930007/), c), the District committed $515 million in 2024 ([DC CFO fiscal impact statement](https://app.cfo.dc.gov/services/fiscal_impact/pdf/spring09/FIS%20Downtown%20Arena%20Revitalization.pdf), b), and a December 2025 stake sale reportedly valued the company at $7.2 billion ([Sportico](https://www.sportico.com/business/team-sales/2025/monumental-wizards-qatar-arctos-powell-jobs-1234879304/), c, unverified). Winning the deals costs owners little: Portland's team reported $100,000 of lobbying in the quarter Oregon approved up to $365 million of state bonds ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/04/20/trail-blazers-moda-center-portland-basketball-sports/), c), pro-arena campaigns outspent opponents 34 to 48 times in the two arena votes held since 2015 (a, from the [arena vote data](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/votes_lobbying/arena_votes.csv), c), San Antonio's city-paid district study came from a subsidiary of the company that owns 20% of the Spurs ([San Antonio Current](https://www.sacurrent.com/?p=377739), c), and Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and the adviser PFM recur on the debt (Table 3). The public has put **$11.15 billion of money and tax breaks into the 30 current arenas in 2026 dollars, plus about $1.53 billion of federal cost from tax-exempt bonds** ([arena ledger totals](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/ledger/totals.txt), a), while the average team value rose from **$2.37 billion in 2020 to $6.64 billion in 2026** ([Sportico valuation data](https://d3data.sportico.com/NBAValuations/NBAValuations2026.csv), c) and the Lakers went from a $10 billion sale in 2025 ([ESPN via ABC7](https://abc7.com/16783698/), c) to an agreed $12.5 billion in 2026 ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/nba/news/lakers-sold-josh-kushner-bob-iger/), c); none of the 30 arena deals reviewed gives the public a share of a team sale or equity in a team (Memphis's lease could not be read). Federal taxpayers add a layer that local deals ignore: a buyer writes off about 90% of a team's price over 15 years, which for Portland's new owner is worth an estimated **$1.07 billion of federal tax over 15 years before any recapture, nearly twice the $573 million** the state, city and county pledged to his arena (a, from the [amortization model](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/tax_mechanisms/nba_control_sales_sec197.csv) and [Portland Resolution 37750](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/resolution/adopted/37750), b). The sale-share clauses that have ever paid in U.S. sports returned less than 1% of a sale price (a, Table 6), so the remedy worth pursuing is a gain-sharing clause that also catches minority, fund and staged transfers, which Portland, San Antonio and Memphis can still write into documents now being negotiated.

## Eleven financiers and four funds now reach 16 of 30 teams

### The league opened to funds in steps and shares in one of them

The NBA did not simply admit outside money; it went into business with it. In 2020 the league formed a partnership with Dyal HomeCourt, now Blue Owl HomeCourt, and Blue Owl's annual report states that **"the NBA provides certain services with respect to the Blue Owl HomeCourt Fund and receives a share of management fees and incentive allocations attributable to the fund"** ([Blue Owl 10-K for 2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1823945/000182394526000009/owl-20251231.htm), b). Blue Owl calls the fund "the only pre-approved institutional investor that can buy equity stakes in all 30 NBA franchises" ([Blue Owl release, July 6, 2026](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/OWL/blue-owl-s-home-court-partners-fund-announces-minority-equity-7fv3ubpu2vaq.html), b). Rules for every other fund followed in January 2021: no fund may own more than 20% of a team or stakes in more than five teams ([Sportico via Yahoo, Feb. 8, 2021](https://sports.yahoo.com/dyal-said-term-sheets-ready-192605642.html), c), all funds together may own no more than 30% of a team, and fund stakes carry no governance rights ([Front Office Sports, Oct. 22, 2025](https://frontofficesports.com/the-nbas-expanding-private-equity-footprint/), c). A sponsor needs $750 million of assets and may put no more than 25% of a fund into one team ([Clifford Chance, Jan. 5, 2026](https://www.cliffordchance.com/content/dam/cliffordchance/briefings/2026/01/2026%20-%201%20From%20Five%20to%20Eight%20-%20NBA%20Further%20Loosens%20Private%20Equity%20Ownership%20Rules.pdf), c). In late 2022 the Board of Governors admitted sovereign wealth funds, pension funds and endowments under the same caps, saying "human rights" and "geopolitical standing" would be weighed ([Duane Morris, Dec. 8, 2022](https://blogs.duanemorris.com/sportslaw/2022/12/08/nba-to-permit-sovereign-wealth-fund-investors-in-franchises-as-valuations-soar/), c), and the 2023 labor contract let players invest up to 5% through an approved fund (Clifford Chance, c). The per-fund limit rose from five teams to **eight, effective December 3, 2025**, after Arctos became "the first to meet the prior limit" (Clifford Chance, c). In September 2026 the league was reported to be rewriting its rules so a firm and its own executives may invest in the same team, as Josh Kushner's Thrive and Bob Iger plan to in the Lakers, provided the firm holds no other NBA interest ([Private Equity Wire, Sept. 21, 2026](https://www.privateequitywire.co.uk/nba-changes-pe-rules-ahead-of-proposed-12-5bn-lakers-acquisition/), c). The two most recent loosenings tracked specific buyers' needs, and the league has not published its reasons or its rule text; every rule above comes from filings, law-firm briefings or press. Institutional money is not new to the league at the top: Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan owned 79.53% of the Raptors' parent until selling to Rogers and Bell for C$1.32 billion in 2012 ([OTPP](https://www.otpp.com/en-ca/about-us/news-and-insights/2012/teachers-completes-1-32-billion-sale-of-mlse/), b), and Platinum Equity co-owned the Pistons from 2011 to 2015 ([Forbes, Tom Gores](https://www.forbes.com/profile/tom-gores/), c).

The one realized exit found, HomeCourt's Suns stake, returned 2.58x on valuation in about 18 months (a, from Sportico via Yahoo, c). Paper multiples overstate fund returns, since they ignore fees, carried interest, minority discounts and debt. ### Eleven controlling owners made their money in finance

Ownership at the top has moved the same way. Using Forbes's description of each owner's source of wealth, **11 of 30 controlling owners, or 37%, made their money in finance** (a): six in private equity or hedge funds and five in mortgage lending, asset management, financial services or venture capital (Table 2). Approval of Marc Stad in Minnesota would make 12 (a). Borderline cases are not counted, including Oklahoma City's George Kaiser, whose fortune is in oil and BOK Financial ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/profile/george-kaiser/), c), and Washington's Ted Leonsis, whose venture firm came after his AOL fortune ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/profile/theodore-leonsis/), c). ### The money reaches public deals through value, operations and land

No record found shows a fund negotiating an arena deal; fund stakes are passive by rule. The ties run through timing and economics instead. **Eight of the 11 fund-held teams have had public arena money approved since 2014**: Atlanta, Charlotte, Cleveland, Minnesota, Sacramento, San Antonio, Utah and Washington, and Memphis would make nine of 12 (a, from the deal records cited in Table 2 and below). A finance-background owner controlled the team, or had signed to buy it, when public money passed in Milwaukee (2015), Atlanta (2016), Cleveland (2017 and after), Detroit (2017) and Portland (2026) (Table 2). Fund money was already inside the ownership group before the latest public deal in four places: San Antonio, where Sixth Street came in during June 2021 and Bexar County voters approved up to $311 million in November 2025 ([Bexar County](https://www.bexar.org/Archive.aspx?ADID=1520), b; [San Antonio term sheet](https://www.sa.gov/files/assets/main/v/1/initiatives/sports-amp-entertainment-district/termsheet.pdf), b); Utah, where Arctos joined in August 2022 before the 2024 state law and city sales tax ([Utah public notice](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/files/1261183.pdf), b); Washington, where the Qatar fund bought in during June 2023 ([AP via WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/sports/2023/06/22/qatar-investment-authority-is-buying-a-stake-in-monumental-sports-entertainment-ap-source-says/), c), nine months before the District's 2024 deal ([DC CFO fiscal impact statement](https://app.cfo.dc.gov/services/fiscal_impact/pdf/spring09/FIS%20Downtown%20Arena%20Revitalization.pdf), b); and Charlotte, where hedge-fund partners joined in 2019 ([NBA.com](https://www.nba.com/article/2019/09/14/jordan-hornets-new-partners-retains-majority-official-release), b) before the city's June 2022 deal ([WBTV](https://www.wbtv.com/2022/06/01/city-pay-275-million-renovate-spectrum-center-fund-new-practice-facility/), c). Elsewhere the funds bought in after the public money was approved. In Portland the tie is written into law: SB 1501 conditions the state bonds on the change in ownership being finalized ([SB 1501](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2026R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/SB1501/Enrolled), b).

Three channels connect the funds to the public money. The first is **value**. Every return source Blue Owl names rises when the public carries arena costs, and Washington's finance office put it plainly: the publicly funded renovation is "likely increasing revenue and long-term value for Monumental," and "the primary purpose of the Arena is to serve Monumental's affiliated businesses, which have significantly larger valuations than the estimated tax liability" ([DC CFO tax abatement analysis](https://cfo.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/ocfo/publication/attachments/TAFA_Capital%20One%20Arena%20112624.pdf), b). From the Qatar fund's entry at $4.05 billion to the reported $7.2 billion valuation 30 months later is a 1.78x paper gain (a, inputs above; the later valuation unverified, and the two figures may not measure the same thing). The second is **operations**. Sixth Street, with 20% of the Spurs and a reported 12.5% of the Celtics, is the majority investor in Legends, whose ASM Global unit manages Oklahoma City's arena and its replacement, and Minneapolis's Target Center according to PFM's comparables summary for Oregon ([Oregon DAS](https://www.oregon.gov/das/Docs/moda-center-arena-comparable-structure-overview.pdf), b); Legends's consulting arm wrote San Antonio's district study (next section). The fees on those contracts were not found. The third is **land**. Owners now seek fund partners for "financing multi-use real estate developments," partly because leagues restrict borrowing ([Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/content/dam/cliffordchance/briefings/2026/01/2026%20-%201%20From%20Five%20to%20Eight%20-%20NBA%20Further%20Loosens%20Private%20Equity%20Ownership%20Rules.pdf), c), and KKR-owned Arctos formed a platform for sports-anchored districts in 2026, starting with a $288 million district next to the University of Tennessee's stadium ([Bloomberg Tax](https://news.bloombergtax.com/private-equity/kkr-owned-arctos-backs-288-million-tennessee-stadium-district), c, unverified).

One test cuts the other way. The 16 teams with a finance owner or a fund investor play in arenas holding **$4.73 billion, or 51%, of the $9.30 billion of core public money**, close to their 53% share of teams; with the Grizzlies it is 17 teams, $5.29 billion and 57% (a, from the per-arena totals in the [arena ledger](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/ledger/arena_ledger.csv)). Most of that money predates both the funds and the new owners. Finance ownership has not, so far, brought more public money; it has bought into teams whose earlier subsidies were already reflected in their prices, and it stands to gain as the next round of public money arrives. Still unknown: current stake sizes for Arctos in the Warriors, Grizzlies, Monumental and SEG, the Qatar fund's stake after December 2025, HomeCourt's stakes in the Timberwolves, Hornets and Cavaliers, Sixth Street's Celtics stake, and who the limited partners are. A Kentucky retirement board document mentioning Arctos could not be downloaded, and the Alaska Permanent Fund appears as an Arctos investor only in search results (unverified), so whether public pensions in arena-subsidizing states own pieces of these funds remains open.

## Cheap lobbying, lopsided campaigns and repeat bankers carry the deals

Owners set the clock and a familiar cast does the rest. Of 20 documented owner or league relocation threats since 1994, 13 were followed by public money in the same city, a median 11 months later ([threat comparison](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/threats/comparison.txt), a). Economists model the result as a bargain in which "the better the team's outside option, the larger the subsidy" (Humphreys and Zhou, as reviewed in [Bradbury, Coates and Humphreys](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/joes.12533), b). Around each owner stand the same kinds of actors: former government staff working as lobbyists, chambers of commerce that run campaigns and commission studies, construction and real estate firms that fund the campaigns, consultants from the venue industry, and banks and advisers whose pay depends on a deal closing.

### Reported lobbying is tiny next to what it wins

Rip City Management, the Trail Blazers' arena company, reported **$100,000 of lobbying in the first quarter of 2026**, the quarter of the short session that passed SB 1501 ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/04/20/trail-blazers-moda-center-portland-basketball-sports/), c). OPB found it was the team's largest quarterly total since at least 2016, spread across eight lobbyists from four firms whose roster included former senior staff of Gov. Tina Kotek and former Republican legislative staff ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/04/20/trail-blazers-moda-center-portland-basketball-sports/), c). Against up to $365 million of state bonds, that is **$3,650 of state money per reported lobbying dollar** (a). The votes came after a side deal: to win Republican support, Democrats lifted the cap on county fairs' share of lottery money, worth about $6.5 million a year from 2027; the bill passed 24-6 and 42-14, and the new owner pays nothing unless the renovation runs over budget ([Willamette Week](https://www.wweek.com/news/state/2026/03/08/oregon-lawmakers-cut-a-bipartisan-deal-to-subsidize-moda-center/), c). The Bucks reported "nearly $700,000" of lobbying in the 2015-16 session that produced about $250 million of public money, about $357 per dollar (a, from [The Badger Project via Wausau Pilot & Review](https://wausaupilotandreview.com/2023/04/23/lobbying-in-wisconsin-seeking-hundreds-of-millions-in-stadium-funding-milwaukee-brewers-have-boosted-lobbying-spending-in-recent-years/), c); the Metropolitan Milwaukee Association of Commerce reported spending 45% of its 2015 lobbying time on the arena ([NationofChange](https://nationofchange.org/2015/08/11/meet-the-lobbyists-who-got-a-250-million-subsidy-for-a-milwaukee-nba-arena), c, an advocacy outlet citing state filings). In Philadelphia, the 76ers' lobbying arm reported almost **$2.3 million** from April 2022 to June 2023, but only about $259,000, or 11.3%, went to direct communication with officials; the rest bought billboards, social media ads and news conferences ([Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/politics/philadelphia/sixers-arena-timeline-pushed-back-philadelphia-city-council-20230914.html), c; share a). Registries therefore understate influence, since team executives, owners' own meetings and public persuasion fall outside them.

Public bodies hire lobbyists too, so public money pays for advocacy to obtain more public money. The Harris County-Houston Sports Authority's July 21, 2026 agenda listed approval of "a consulting contract with Texas Lobby Strategies" next to a Toyota Center renovation update, with no value shown ([HCHSA agenda](https://houstonsports.org/wp-content/uploads/Notice-and-Agenda-BOD-07.21.26.pdf), b); the $180 million overhaul counts on an expected state grant of about $95 million ([The Real Deal](https://therealdeal.com/texas/2026/04/09/toyota-center-in-houston-set-for-180m-overhaul), c). Whether the contract concerns the arena was not established. In Washington, Monumental registered at least six Virginia lobbyists or firms between December 18, 2023 and January 4, 2024 on creating an arena authority for Potomac Yard ([VPAP](https://www.vpap.org/lobbying/client/489386/), c, unverified), then turned to Congress, which reviews the District's budget: its federal filings report $60,000 a quarter to Forbes-Tate from late 2024, citing "funding of the District of Columbia" from 2025, plus $40,000 a quarter to Summit Public Affairs, **about $540,000 through mid-2026** ([House Clerk lobbying disclosures](https://lobbyingdisclosure.house.gov/), b; total a). The league itself barely lobbies Congress: the NBA reported $15,000 of federal lobbying in 2015, when its lobbyist's issue list included "taxation of municipal bonds to include sports stadiums," then filings with no reportable amount through 2022 and none since, against $1.6 million to $3.2 million a year for the NFL from 2015 to 2025 (House Clerk, b). When the House voted in 2025 to halve the write-off for team purchases, the only lobbyist on record on the provision worked for Major League Baseball (House Clerk, b).

The largest owner-linked political money found comes from an owner's other business. Since January 2024 Miriam Adelson, whose family controls the Mavericks, has put $13.2 million into two Texas PACs ([Texas Monthly](https://texasmonthly.com/news-politics/texas-legislature-sands-casinos-gambling-bust), c, unverified), and the Sands interests spent more than $10 million on lobbyists over two sessions without legalizing casinos ([Texas Standard](https://texasstandard.org/stories/dallas-mavericks-sale-mark-cuban-texas-gambling/), c, unverified). Mavericks governor Patrick Dumont says a casino resort is on a separate timeline from the team's planned arena ([Casino.org](https://casino.org/news/sands-ceo-dumont-says-mavs-arena-plans-dont-include-casino), c, unverified). No lobbying amounts were found for the deals in Salt Lake City, Memphis, Oklahoma City, Charlotte, Orlando, Atlanta or Sacramento, and most state and city registries were not queried.

### Pro-arena campaigns outspent opponents 34 to 48 times

Only two arena funding votes were held in the period, and the money was lopsided in both. In Oklahoma City's December 12, 2023 sales-tax election, the Greater Oklahoma City Chamber's "Keep OKC Big League" campaign spent at least $478,000 against less than $10,000 for opponents, **about 48 to 1**, and the tax passed with 71% ([arena vote data](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/votes_lobbying/arena_votes.csv), c; ratio a); the same chamber commissioned the campaign's economic study ([Field of Schemes](https://www.fieldofschemes.com/2023/11/16/20614/okc-consultants-say-thunder-arena-is-huge-boon-to-economy-its-not-building-new-900m-one-would-be-even-more-so-it-wouldnt/), c). In Bexar County's November 4, 2025 Proposition B, the Spurs-backed Win Together PAC raised $9.2 million against about $270,000, **about 34 to 1**, and the measure passed with 52.1% ([San Antonio Report](https://sanantonioreport.org/?p=5426832), c; [KSAT](https://www.ksat.com/news/local/2025/11/04/results-props-a-and-b-for-coliseum-complex-downtown-spurs-arena-in-the-nov-4-2025-election/), c; ratio a). Most of the money came from San Antonio Spurs LLC, with large checks from Guido Construction and Sundt; nearly every well-known local political consultant was paid, and $2.3 million arrived in the days before the vote ([San Antonio Report](https://sanantonioreport.org/?p=5426832), c). Before the late spending, a UTSA poll had 46% of likely voters opposed and 40% in favor ([San Antonio Current](https://www.sacurrent.com/news/san-antonio-news/arena-pac-spending/), c, unverified).

Elsewhere the route was to keep the decision away from voters. Phoenix's clerk refused referendum petitions in 2019 because city attorneys said the council's deal was not a "legislative act" ([Arena Digest](https://arenadigest.com/2019/02/06/group-drops-talking-stick-resort-arena-referendum-pursuit/), c, unverified), after a December 2018 poll found 66% of likely voters opposed ([Sports Business Daily](https://www.sportsbusinessdaily.com/Daily/Morning-Buzz/2019/01/24.aspx), c, unverified). Sacramento's clerk rejected a 2014 petition over differences between circulated versions ([Arena Digest](https://arenadigest.com/2014/01/27/new-sacramento-arena-financial-impact-11-5-billion-city/), c). In Cleveland, after the Ohio Supreme Court ruled in 2017 that the arena deal must go to a vote, petitioners withdrew in exchange for a county study of mental-health crisis centers ([WKYC](https://wkyc.com/article/news/local/cleveland/greater-cleveland-congregations-reaches-deal-to-withdraw-quicken-loans-arena-deal-petitions/95-469647127), c; [Ideastream](https://ideastream.org/news/community/2017-04-26/what-could-happen-if-voters-reject-clevelands-q-renovation-funding), c), and the lease now runs through 2033-34 ([Gateway audit](https://ohioauditor.gov/auditsearch/Reports/2023/Gateway_Economic_Development_Corporation_of_Greater_Cleveland_22-Cuyahoga_FINAL.pdf), b).

### Studies come from parties with a stake in the answer

The impact studies used to sell these deals were paid for by the team, by the chamber running the campaign or by the city, and they count gross spending with multipliers rather than net gains. San Antonio shows how the interests overlap. A Spurs-employed consultant, Stone Planning, spent about six months with team officials on a 29-page analysis projecting about 600,000 more attendees, about 100 direct jobs and $225 million of added direct spending a year, $318 million with federal multipliers ([San Antonio Report](https://sanantonioreport.org/spurs-economic-analysis-stone-csl-study/), c). The city hired CSL International for the district study, which relied heavily on Stone's arena numbers (same source, c) and projected $18.7 billion of output and $351 million more in city taxes over 30 years (unverified). **CSL is a subsidiary of Legends, which is owned by Sixth Street, the Spurs' 20% investor**; Mayor Gina Ortiz Jones said the study "may have been clouded by conflict of interest" ([San Antonio Current](https://www.sacurrent.com/?p=377739), c). The council voted down her motion for more due diligence 4-7 and approved the term sheet 7-4 ([Bond Buyer](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/san-antonio-oks-negotiations-for-spurs-arena), c), while economist Brandli Stitzel said "the net economic impact of sports teams and stadiums on local economies is effectively zero" ([San Antonio Report](https://sanantonioreport.org/spurs-economic-analysis-stone-csl-study/), c). The city's later hires price costs and manage delivery rather than test the benefits: MuniCap at $350,000 and Accenture at $6 million for the first year, both paid from hotel tax ([San Antonio Report](https://sanantonioreport.org/bids-project-marvel-consulting-firms-san-antonio-council-approval/), c).

Other cities follow the pattern. Oklahoma City's chamber hired Applied Economics, which estimated $590 million a year of impact and more than 3,000 jobs ([Field of Schemes](https://www.fieldofschemes.com/2023/11/16/20614/okc-consultants-say-thunder-arena-is-huge-boon-to-economy-its-not-building-new-900m-one-would-be-even-more-so-it-wouldnt/), c). Portland's August 2026 resolution leans on a 2023 city-commissioned study by Crossroads (more than $600 million a year and nearly 6,000 jobs), saying newer data is "not available" and that it is "reasonable to assume similar annual economic impacts" ([Resolution 37750](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/resolution/adopted/37750), b). Memphis's mayor cited "more than $21 billion over 15 years" from Younger & Associates ([WREG](https://wreg.com/news/local/it-puts-us-in-another-league-memphis-proposing-684m-sports-upgrade-new-soccer-stadium/amp/), c). Sacramento's $11.5 billion, 35-year projection, about half of it from businesses that did not yet exist, was commissioned by "a political group tied to the mayor and the Kings" ([Arena Digest](https://arenadigest.com/2014/01/27/new-sacramento-arena-financial-impact-11-5-billion-city/), c). The Pistons' owner commissioned a University of Michigan study ($596.2 million of impact) for the move downtown ([Bond Buyer](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/new-money-deal-will-finance-detroit-arena-adaptation), c), and Philadelphia's city-commissioned studies of the 76ers' proposed arena were paid for by the team ([Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/politics/philadelphia/76ers-arena-impact-study-takeaways-20240828.html), c). Fees were found only for Phoenix's arena consultant, Barrett Sports Group, at $200,000 (a, from [AZBEX](https://dev.azbex.com/tag/america-west-arena/), c). In no case found did a public body commission an independent net-impact study before voting.

### Land and districts beside public arenas go to owners

Owners increasingly come away with land or development rights next to publicly funded arenas, and no deal reviewed returns any of that land value to the public. Milwaukee County sold the Bucks 10 acres of former freeway land for **$1** in 2015, against an appraisal of "nearly $9 million" that the county said ignored removal costs ([WPR](https://wpr.org/economy/milwaukee-county-sells-land-bucks-arena-development-1), c), the start of the Deer District. In Salt Lake City, $375 million of the $900 million of sales-tax bonds pays for the district rather than the arena ([Utah public notice](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/files/1261183.pdf), b); three of the five district trustees are executives of the owner's company, and two of them moved and seconded the district's reimbursement agreement with SEG Real Estate ([district minutes](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/files/1368579.pdf), b); and SEG agreed to buy 6.5 acres of county convention-center land east of the arena for $55.4 million, about $8.5 million an acre, closing in early 2027 ([Deseret News](https://www.deseret.com/utah/2026/10/08/ryan-smith-launches-survey-in-search-for-strong-local-vibe-at-slc-entertainment-district), c; per acre a). Detroit's council staff put the Olympia and Related brownfield plan for the District Detroit at **$616.3 million of tax capture over 35 years**, 40% of the $1.53 billion of stated investment, including $138.1 million of school operating tax ([Detroit Legislative Policy Division](https://detroitmi.gov/sites/detroitmi.localhost/files/2023-02/DPS%20and%20Library%20Exemption%20for%20the%20District%20Detroit%20TBP%20ms.pdf), b; share a), although the 2014 pitch had promised 686 apartments and a 2021 review found no promised residential project completed ([Deadline Detroit](https://www.deadlinedetroit.com/articles/28167/ilitches_empty_promises_about_district_detroit_housing_spark_another_story), c, unverified).

San Antonio's term sheet ties city bonds to $1.4 billion of taxable development, with the Spurs and developers guaranteeing the tax-increment revenue ([term sheet](https://www.sa.gov/files/assets/main/v/1/initiatives/sports-amp-entertainment-district/termsheet.pdf), b). On September 10, 2026 the council let the Midtown tax-increment zone borrow up to $60 million to buy the arena site from the University of Texas System, at a cost that "could end up" above $115 million with financing, and extended the zone's life from 2041 to 2060 ([KSAT](https://www.ksat.com/news/local/2026/09/11/council-approves-dollar60m-in-tirz-debt-to-buy-land-for-spurs-arena/), c). Oklahoma City gave the Thunder's owners the exclusive right to buy the old arena site at fair market value within five years of the new arena opening ([OK Business Voice](https://okbusinessvoice.com/2025/06/18/oklahoma-city-council-approves-agreements-to-keep-thunder-in-okc/), c). The Mavericks hold options on more than 100 acres at Dallas's Valley View site, inside a tax-increment district whose owner says a deal could make "over $100 million in tax increment available" to a mall in southern Dallas ([Candy's Dirt](https://candysdirt.com/2026/06/01/dallas-mavericks-enter-option-agreement-with-valley-view-owners/), c). Sacramento's 2014 deal gave the Kings $32 million of city land and six digital billboards ([Pollstar](https://news.pollstar.com/2014/05/21/sacramento-approves-arena/), c). In Washington, Monumental leased nearly half of the adjacent Gallery Place after it sold for $39 million against a $225.7 million assessment ([Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2025/01/monumental-sports-75ksf-lease-dc/), c, unverified). The counterexample is Portland's August 2026 term sheet: "The City retains development rights for the Arena Site and all adjacent City owned parcels," and Multnomah County asked that rights the current agreement gave the Blazers go instead to the Albina Vision Trust ([Resolution 37750](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/resolution/adopted/37750), b).

Not reached for this section: the state lobbying registries for Oregon, Utah, Texas, Tennessee, Wisconsin, California, Indiana and Michigan, DC's and Philadelphia's registries, itemized San Antonio PAC reports (the county portal returned errors), and the fees paid to CSL, Stone, Applied Economics, Crossroads and Younger & Associates.

## Prize-winning reporting exposed owners' taxes and side deals, rarely the arenas

### The prizes followed the money around teams

National prizes have almost never gone to reporting on arena deals themselves. Two Pulitzers touch sports money. The **2026 prize for Audio Reporting went to *Pablo Torre Finds Out*** for reporting that the Clippers routed money to Kawhi Leonard through a $28 million endorsement deal with Aspiration, a now-bankrupt sustainability company in which Steve Ballmer had invested $50 million and which announced a $300 million partnership with the team about a month after Leonard signed a $176 million extension ([AP via News4Jax](https://www.news4jax.com/sports/2026/05/04/pablo-torres-podcast-wins-pulitzer-prize-for-clippers-report-capping-a-remarkable-rise/), c; [Pulitzer citation](https://www.pulitzer.org/news/winning-impact-staff-pablo-torre-finds-out), b, unverified). The 2023 prize for Local Reporting went to Anna Wolfe of Mississippi Today for showing how a former governor steered welfare money to friends including Brett Favre, money that helped pay for a university volleyball facility ([Pulitzer citation](https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/anna-wolfe-mississippi-today-ridgeland-miss), b, unverified). Searches of the Pulitzer site found no winner or finalist for a stadium or arena subsidy investigation (unverified; the site blocked direct access), and Long Island University's list of George Polk Award winners from 1949 to 2025 has none ([LIU](https://liu.edu/polk-awards/past-winners), b).

The awards that did reach this field went to tax and accountability work. ProPublica's "The Secret IRS Files," which included "The Billionaire Playbook," won IRE's top 2021 print and online award ([IRE](https://www.ire.org/past-award-winners/2021-award-winners/), b), a 2021 Sigma Delta Chi award ([SPJ](https://www.spj.org/sigma-delta-chi-awards-2021-honorees/), b) and the 2022 Selden Ring Award ([USC Annenberg](https://annenberg.usc.edu/events/2022-selden-ring-award-ceremony), b). WCPO-TV's 1999 Cincinnati "Stadium Investigation" won a Peabody for uncovering "broken promises, manipulation of numbers in official reports, political cronyism in contract awards" and "pass-through" companies, which led to a state investigation of stadium spending ([Peabody](https://peabodyawards.com/award-profile/stadium-investigation/), b). The Los Angeles Times' media critic David Shaw won a 1999 Sigma Delta Chi award for dissecting his own paper's profit-sharing deal with Staples Center, now the Lakers' Crypto.com Arena ([SPJ](https://www.spj.org/sigma-delta-chi-awards-1999-honorees/), b), and ESPN won a 2024 IRE award for "The NBA, Rwanda and Sportswashing" ([IRE](https://www.ire.org/2025/04/16/announcing-the-2024-ire-award-winners-and-finalists/), b).

### What the investigations found about NBA owners

The NBA findings center on Steve Ballmer, Dan Gilbert and the Ilitch family. ProPublica showed that **Ballmer reported $656 million of income for 2018 and paid $78 million, a 12% federal rate**, and that the Clippers, which audited financials showed earning $14 million and $18 million in the two years before he bought them, **reported $700 million of tax losses**; owning the team "cut his tax bill by about $140 million in just five years" ([ProPublica, July 8, 2021](https://www.propublica.org/article/the-billionaire-playbook-how-sports-owners-use-their-teams-to-avoid-millions-in-taxes), c). **Dan Gilbert lowered his taxable income by about $443 million from 2005 to 2018** through his Cavaliers stake, and Michael Jordan's Hornets stake produced $3.6 million of tax losses in 2015, a year the team was estimated to be profitable (same source, c). The mechanism is the 2004 law that lets buyers amortize the franchise and other intangibles over 15 years, "typically 90% or more of the price," and heirs can generally restart the cycle ([ProPublica takeaways](https://www.propublica.org/article/eight-takeaways-from-propublicas-investigation-of-how-sports-owners-use-their-teams-to-avoid-taxes), c). A 2019 ProPublica story found that a downtown Detroit tract dominated by Gilbert's buildings was added to the Opportunity Zone list although its median family income was almost 1.5 times the limit, less than two weeks after a Michigan official wrote that Quicken Loans "worked with the White House on it" ([ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/how-a-tax-break-to-help-the-poor-went-to-nba-owner-dan-gilbert), c).

The Clippers story moved from podcast to penalty within a year. On September 2, 2026 the NBA found that the team broke its circumvention rules by "affirmatively initiating off-court income opportunities between Mr. Leonard and four companies doing business with the team: Aspiration Partners, Boingo Wireless, Daktronics, and Lockton Insurance" and "inducing the companies to enter into these agreements by offering them business from the team." It **fined the Clippers $30 million, took five first-round picks (2029 to 2033), suspended Ballmer for a year** and placed the team under five years of league monitoring ([NBA](https://pr.nba.com/nba-investigation-clippers-kawhi-leonard/), b). The investigators' summary reported that Leonard's deals with the three smaller vendors were worth $18 million and that the team's payments to those vendors "may in fact have been made principally to fund the endorsement deals" ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/?p=248368), c); the Clippers reject the findings.

Local reporting produced the hardest numbers on arena deals themselves. The Detroit Free Press showed Little Caesars Arena's cost nearly doubled from $450 million to just under $863 million while the public share rose from $262 million to $324 million ([Bond Buyer, from the Free Press](https://www.bondbuyer.com/articles/little-caesars-arena-how-the-cost-nearly-doubled-to-863-million), c), and the Ilitches' Olympia Development later qualified for a **$74 million reimbursement by counting $200 million of "ancillary development" that included two parking decks and a Google office inside the arena** ([Deadline Detroit](https://www.deadlinedetroit.com/articles/22323), c; [Sports Business Daily](https://www.sportsbusinessdaily.com/Daily/Issues/2019/05/13/Facilities/Ilitch.aspx), c). Norman Oder's Atlantic Yards Report traced $577 million of EB-5 visa money raised in three rounds while the Barclays Center was marketed to investors (a: $228M + $249M + $100M, from [Oder](https://normanoder.substack.com/p/did-eb-5-funds-help-build-the-barclays), c), and found the state received minimal spending documentation only in mid-2019, apparently prompted by his records request ([Oder](https://normanoder.substack.com/p/developer-certified-that-eb-5-spending), c). Discovery in Seattle's lease suit produced the SuperSonics owners' emails, including Aubrey McClendon's "We didn't buy the team to keep it in Seattle," which drew a $250,000 NBA fine ([Seattle Times archive](https://archive.seattletimes.com/archive/20080415/schultz15/howard-schultz-plans-to-sue-clay-bennett-to-get-sonics-back), c). The Philadelphia Inquirer showed that the 76ers-funded city studies put net tax revenue from the proposed arena at $390 million over 30 years against the team's $1.5 billion claim ([Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/politics/philadelphia/76ers-arena-impact-study-takeaways-20240828.html), c).

What changed afterward came mostly from a league or from voters. Detroit paid the full rebate. The SEC closed its Marlins stadium-bond inquiry with no action while warning that this "must in no way be construed as indicating that the party has been exonerated" ([Bond Buyer](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/sec-ends-marlins-stadium-probe-with-no-charges), c). Miami-Dade, owed 5% of the Marlins' net sale proceeds, was certified $0 on a $1.2 billion sale and settled for $5.5 million ([Miami-Dade County Attorney](https://www.miamidade.gov/govaction/legistarfiles/MinMatters/Y2021/210580min.pdf), b). Members of Congress asked for an inquiry into Gilbert's Opportunity Zone tract, with no outcome found ([Metro Times](https://www.metrotimes.com/news/legislators-call-for-investigation-after-dan-gilbert-got-a-tax-break-meant-for-impoverished-communities-23040302/), c, unverified). Seattle voters passed Initiative 91 in 2006 with 74%, requiring a "fair value" return on any city investment in pro sports facilities ([Seattle City Council](https://council.seattle.gov/?p=3757), b).

### Tax-exempt bonds have outlasted a decade of repeal attempts

Arena bonds keep their federal tax exemption through a workaround. Since the Tax Reform Act of 1986 a stadium cannot use qualified private activity bonds, but it "nonetheless can be financed with tax-exempt governmental bonds if the debt service is paid from sources other than sports facility revenues or other private payments, such as generally applicable taxes" ([Treasury FY2016 General Explanations, p. 85](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/131/General-Explanations-FY2016.pdf), b). That is why cities pledge sales, hotel and property taxes, or payments in lieu of taxes, rather than arena rent. Treasury called the result "artificial financing structures" that "shifted more of the costs and risks from the private owners to local residents and taxpayers in general," and proposed ending it in the 2016 and 2017 budgets at an estimated **$542 million over ten years** ([FY2016](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/131/General-Explanations-FY2016.pdf); [FY2017](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/131/General-Explanations-FY2017.pdf), b). The House's 2017 tax bill would have taxed interest on new "professional sports stadium" bonds, but "the conference agreement does not follow the House bill provision" ([H. Rept. 115-466](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-115hrpt466/html/CRPT-115hrpt466.htm), b), and the No Tax Subsidies for Stadiums Act has been introduced in at least four Congresses since 2017 without a floor vote ([H.R. 811](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-115hr811ih/html/BILLS-115hr811ih.htm); [H.R. 2446](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-116hr2446ih/html/BILLS-116hr2446ih.htm); [S. 392](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-118s392is/html/BILLS-118s392is.htm); [H.R. 2434](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr2434ih/html/BILLS-119hr2434ih.htm); [S. 1192](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s1192is/html/BILLS-119s1192is.htm), b). Across leagues, 43 of 57 stadiums built since 2000 used tax-exempt bonds, at a **federal revenue loss of $4.3 billion** ([Drukker, Gayer and Gold, National Tax Journal 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ntj/journl/v73y2020i1p157-196.html), b), the figures Ways and Means Chairman Jason Smith cited at a June 30, 2026 hearing where economist Dennis Coates urged an end to the exemption ([hearing summary](https://hlc.com/en/publications/ways-and-means-hearing-shows-continued-bipartisan-interest-in-removing-tax-breaks-for-sports-teams), c; [Coates testimony](https://waysandmeans.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Coates-Ways-and-Means-Testimony.pdf), b). For NBA arenas alone the federal cost of the new money in 25 issues is about **$1.53 billion** in present value ([arena ledger](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/ledger/totals.txt), a), including about $7.9 million a year for the arena's share of Salt Lake City's 2025 district bonds ([arena report](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/report/who-paid-for-nba-arenas.md), a).

### Each control sale restarts a 15-year write-off

The second federal channel is the deduction for buying a team. The American Jobs Creation Act of 2004 extended 15-year amortization of intangibles to sports franchises ([Pub. L. 108-357, sec. 886](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-108publ357/html/PLAW-108publ357.htm), b), so a buyer can deduct the franchise, media contracts and other intangibles, "typically 90% or more of the price," over 15 years ([ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/the-billionaire-playbook-how-sports-owners-use-their-teams-to-avoid-millions-in-taxes), c). In May 2025 the House voted to cut the deduction in half, explaining that "the owners of sports teams unfairly benefit from the amortization of intangibles" and that the deductions "may allow sports team owners to offset losses attributable to a sports team against other income" ([H. Rept. 119-106](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-119hrpt106/html/CRPT-119hrpt106-pt2.htm), b; [engrossed H.R. 1](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr1eh/html/BILLS-119hr1eh.htm), b). The Joint Committee on Taxation put the change at about $991 million over ten years; the Senate draft of June 16, 2025 dropped it, and the law signed July 4, 2025 does not contain it ([Bloomberg Tax](https://news.bloombergtax.com/daily-tax-report/sports-team-owners-write-off-spared-in-senate-tax-bill), c). No stated reason was found.

At the model's assumptions (90% of the price amortizable over 15 years, 37% top rate), **each $1 billion paid for a team yields $60 million a year of deductions worth about $22 million a year in federal tax**, about $333 million over 15 years (a). Portland's March 2026 purchase, 80.1% at a $4.0 billion valuation ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/03/30/nba-approves-trail-blazers-sale-tom-dundon/), c), works out to $192.2 million a year of deductions and $71.1 million a year of tax value: **$1.07 billion over 15 years, or $738 million in present value at 5%, against $573 million of state, city and county renovation money** ([amortization model](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/tax_mechanisms/nba_control_sales_sec197.csv), a; [Resolution 37750](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/resolution/adopted/37750), b). These are estimates of tax value at the top rate, not of net federal cost: amortization is recaptured as ordinary income on a later sale unless the owner dies holding the team, and price allocations and owners' tax positions are not public. Turnover feeds the subsidy, because each sale at a higher price starts a new 15-year write-off at the new basis; the Lakers' agreed $12.5 billion resale ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/nba/news/lakers-sold-josh-kushner-bob-iger/), c) would restart it a year after Walter's purchase at a $10 billion valuation ([ESPN via ABC7](https://abc7.com/16783698/), c) (inference from the law). The federal and local layers fall on the same owners: Gilbert's Cavaliers stake lowered his taxable income by about $443 million from 2005 to 2018 (ProPublica, c), and in 2017 the public put about $70 million of principal into renovating his team's publicly owned arena ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/local/cleveland/2023/02/06/cleveland-cavs-impact), c). No arena deal reviewed takes an owner's federal tax benefits into account.

### Pandemic aid, Opportunity Zones, EB-5 and grants were small or cannot be counted

Pandemic programs mostly missed NBA arena companies, but three operators tied to billionaire owners or their joint ventures took the maximum. The Shuttered Venue Operators Grant paid 45% of 2019 gross earned revenue up to $10 million and excluded listed companies and large multistate operators ([15 U.S.C. 9009a](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/9009a), b). Four NBA arena operators received **$31.48 million** in all (a): Larry H. Miller Arena Corporation, the Delta Center building company Ryan Smith had bought with the Jazz in December 2020, $10 million in July 2021 ([USAspending](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/ASST_NON_SBAHQ21SV001805_073), b; [KSL](https://www.ksl.com/article/50069555/nba-board-of-governors-approves-sale-of-utah-jazz-to-ryan-smith), c); Center Operating Company, the Mavericks and Stars joint venture running the American Airlines Center, $10 million ([USAspending](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/ASST_NON_SBAHQ21SV010114_073), b); the City of Orlando, operator of the Kia Center and other venues, $10 million ([SBA award file](https://data.sba.gov/sites/default/files/distribution/SBA-ODA-2022-09-001/awards-as-of-7-5-22.xlsx), b); and the Wisconsin Center District, owner of Fiserv Forum, $1.48 million, most likely for its other venues ([USAspending](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/ASST_NON_SBAHQ21SV009074_073), b). The Lakers took about $4.6 million from the Paycheck Protection Program in April 2020 and returned it ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/l-lakers-received-4-6-million-federal-loan-program-returned-n1193491), c); the federal record now shows a $0 loan ([USAspending](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/ASST_NON_1652897101_073), b), and a scan of all 968,524 PPP loans over $150,000 found no loan to another NBA team, the league or an NBA arena company (a, from [SBA's PPP data](https://data.sba.gov/dataset/ppp-foia), b), though a company borrowing under an unrelated holding-company name would not show up.

Opportunity Zones and EB-5 visas cluster around arenas, but the money behind them cannot be counted. **Thirteen of the 30 current arenas sit in tracts designated in 2018** (Cleveland, Detroit, Houston, Indianapolis, Memphis, New Orleans, Oklahoma City, Orlando, Phoenix, Portland, Sacramento, San Antonio and Salt Lake City), and so do the points tested for San Antonio's new arena, Oklahoma City's new arena, Salt Lake City's convention-center district, Milwaukee's Deer District and the District Detroit (a, from the [CDFI Fund list](https://www.cdfifund.gov/system/files/documents/designated-qozs.12.14.18.xlsx), b, as tested in the [site file](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/property_tax/oz_arena_sites_combined.csv)). The program has no investor disclosure, so tax savings cannot be measured ([ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/how-a-tax-break-to-help-the-poor-went-to-nba-owner-dan-gilbert), c). Beyond Gilbert's Detroit tract, the documented owner case is RevOZ, the fund of a Kings minority-owner family, which bought a downtown Sacramento building from a Kings ownership affiliate ([AltsWire](https://altswire.com/revoz-argosy-jv-closes-opportunity-zone-investment-in-downtown-sacramento/), c). EB-5, which trades green-card eligibility for low-cost loans, touched two NBA arena projects: the first Atlantic Yards round raised $228 million (initially $249 million) under the name "Brooklyn Arena and Transportation Infrastructure" and was repaid by January 2020, though whether any dollar reached the arena is disputed and Oder concludes it did not ([Oder](https://normanoder.substack.com/p/did-eb-5-funds-help-build-the-barclays), c); and an EB-5 regional center pitched Chinese investors in 2015 on Golden 1 Center and Downtown Commons "being built by" the Kings without stating an amount ([Golden Gate Global](https://www.davidperry.com/newsroom/golden-gate-global-presents-a-championship-season-for-eb-5-investment.html), c).

Direct federal grants for NBA arenas are rare. The only construction-era grant found is the $12 million of federal money for the Metro connection at Washington's arena in 1997 ([GAO](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-98-223/pdf/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-98-223.pdf), b). The largest federal commitment near an NBA arena ran the other way: the Federal Transit Administration indicated a **$1,009,430,998 grant**, half the cost, for a 1.6-mile people mover linking Metro to the Kia Forum, SoFi Stadium and the Intuit Dome ([Progressive Railroading](https://www.progressiverailroading.com/passenger_rail/news/FTA-set-to-award-1B-grant-to-Inglewood-Transit-Connector--70977), c), but Steve Ballmer and Stan Kroenke "had once strongly supported the project" and cooled when designs put the line across their properties ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/los-angeles/construction-development/with-major-gap-in-funding-inglewood-people-movers-future-uncertain-126505), c), and the city shelved it for bus and bike lanes in April 2025 ([Construction Dive](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/inglewood-people-mover-on-hold/746477/), c). Federal agencies paid NBA team companies $2,849,342 under marketing, recruiting and ticket contracts from FY2008 to FY2025, led by Kroenke Sports & Entertainment at $1.51 million, mostly Army social-media and advertising work, and the Pacers at $862,900, including $145,000 for "Pacers NBA2K gaming" with the Indiana Army National Guard ([USAspending, Kroenke 2022](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_W912LC22P0031_9700_-NONE-_-NONE-); [USAspending, Pacers 2018](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_W912L918P0063_9700_-NONE-_-NONE-), b; totals a). Not found or not reachable: the Joint Committee on Taxation's own tables, investor-level Opportunity Zone data (not public), EB-5 project totals (not published by USCIS), employee retention credits, bonus depreciation on privately built arenas, and transit grants near other arenas.

## Team values nearly tripled while the public's contractual share stayed at zero

### None of the 30 deals shares a sale or a stake

The 30 NBA teams were worth a combined **$71.14 billion in Sportico's 2020 valuations and $199.26 billion in 2026**, a gain of about $128 billion in six years, or 18.7% a year on average ([Sportico data](https://d3data.sportico.com/NBAValuations/NBAValuations2026.csv), c; growth a). The 30 Forbes team pages for 2026 average $6.13 billion (a, from [Forbes team pages](https://www.forbes.com/teams/golden-state-warriors/), c). The Lakers' price rose 25% between a $10 billion agreement in June 2025 and a $12.5 billion agreement in August 2026 (a, from [ABC7](https://abc7.com/16783698/) and [CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/nba/news/lakers-sold-josh-kushner-bob-iger/), c), though the Lakers' arena involved no counted public money, so they show league-wide appreciation rather than a subsidy deal. Against that rise, **none of the 30 current arena deals reviewed contains a sale-proceeds clause or gives the public equity in a team** (a, from the leases, term sheets, audits and press accounts reviewed for each arena, including those cited below); Memphis's 2001 lease and its pending replacement could not be read. Transfers are allowed without payment. Oklahoma City's 2025 license lets the team pass to any NBA-approved buyer without public consent ([license agreement, s14.3](https://www.okc.gov/files/assets/city/v/1/city-manager/documents/pbcarena-use-license-agreementfinal-6-12-25-1.pdf), b); Houston's lease lets the tenant sell the franchise "without the consent of Landlord" ([Toyota Center lease, s15.4](https://houstonsports.org/wp-content/uploads/Toyota_Center_-_Arena_Lease.pdf), b); and Utah's participation agreement lets SEG sell interests in the Jazz, including control, with league approval ([draft agreement, s12.3](https://www.slcdocs.com/council/WebDoc/CapitalCityRevitalizationZone/UpdatedDraftParticipationAgreement070524.pdf), b), although the state law behind it said the agreement "may contain ... revenue sharing" ([SB 272](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/sbillint/SB0272S02.htm), b). Indianapolis holds a right of first refusal on a controlling-interest sale, not a share of the gain ([CIB FY2023 audit](https://www.in.gov/sboa/WebReports/83950A.pdf), b). In six control sales since 2019 of teams playing in publicly funded arenas (Brooklyn, Minnesota, Phoenix, Charlotte, Dallas and Portland), no payment to the public owner was found (a, from the lease and press records cited in this section and Table 2).

What the public gets back is mostly fixed. Only Phoenix (a variable fee tied to operations, about $4 million a year after 2019), Orlando (where the city runs the arena and keeps revenue from other events, while the Magic pay fixed naming and suite amounts rising 3% a year) and Portland (a proposed 6% fee on Rose Quarter tickets) have any payment that moves with operations or prices ([Phoenix council report](https://webapi.legistar.com/v1/phoenix/matters/10618/texts/10724), b; [Orlando interlocal agreement](https://occompt.legistar1.com/occompt/attachments/017457ff-5195-4c2a-907c-792707710a8e.pdf), b; [Resolution 37750](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/resolution/adopted/37750), b). Profit shares that existed paid little or nothing and then disappeared. Miami-Dade's 40% of arena net cash flow above $14 million paid nothing through FY2011, paid $257,134 once in 2013 and was deleted in 2014 for a $1 million-a-year donation ([Miami-Dade Inspector General](https://www.miamidadeig.org/resources-oig/pdf/Reports2012/IG11.34FinalBasketball.pdf), b). Bexar County's 20% of the Spurs arena's net operating income above a threshold never paid while the operator booked $82 million of losses ([Express-News via Headliners Foundation](https://headlinersfoundation.org/showcase-awards/2015/spurs-arena-delivers-no-profit-sharing-to-the-county), c). Cleveland's 1994 rent, a share of suite, club-seat and parking revenue, was replaced in 2017 by cost-based rent that covers the public owner's operating budget and repays the team's half of the renovation ([Gateway audit](https://ohioauditor.gov/auditsearch/Reports/2023/Gateway_Economic_Development_Corporation_of_Greater_Cleveland_22-Cuyahoga_FINAL.pdf), b). The partial exception is Portland, where part of Multnomah County's $88 million is "contingent upon receipt of Business Income Taxes realized from the sale of the franchise" ([Resolution 37750](https://www.portland.gov/council/documents/resolution/adopted/37750), b), though another exhibit of the same term sheet names motor-vehicle-rental and tourism taxes instead.

### Why the gain lands with owners

The research explains where the money goes. A 2023 review of more than 130 studies found "the decades-old consensus of very limited economic impacts," with welfare gains that "fall well short of covering public outlays" ([Bradbury, Coates and Humphreys](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/joes.12533), b), and Coates told Ways and Means in June 2026 that subsidies are "largely redistributive," moving income "from taxpayers to professional athletes and team owners, from the relatively poor to the relatively wealthy" ([Coates testimony](https://waysandmeans.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Coates-Ways-and-Means-Testimony.pdf), b). Twenty to 50% of each additional $1 million of subsidy goes into a more lavish building ([Propheter 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1527002514551803), b, as summarized in the review), and a new stadium raises franchise values "regardless of how construction was financed" ([Miller](https://ideas.repec.org/p/spe/wpaper/0626.html), b; also [Alexander and Kern](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/jospec/v5y2004i1p51-66.html), b). The NBA's labor contract keeps the capital subsidy away from players: Basketball Related Income excludes "any thing of value received in connection with design/construction of a new or renovated arena" and payments meant to induce a team "to relocate to or remain" ([2023 CBA, Art. VII](https://imgix.cosmicjs.com/25da5eb0-15eb-11ee-b5b3-fbd321202bdf-Final-2023-NBA-Collective-Bargaining-Agreement-6-28-23.pdf), b). Public money that an owner would otherwise have spent therefore becomes owner equity, realized at the next sale and untaxed by the host city unless the deal says otherwise (inference). Official totals understate the transfer as well: omitted land, infrastructure, services and forgone property taxes can raise public obligations 25% to 40% above reported costs ([Bradbury, Coates and Humphreys retrospective](https://campus.kennesaw.edu/colleges-departments/coles/centers/markets-economic-opportunity/docs/bradbury-coates-humphreys-01-30-2023.pdf), b).

Fund ownership changes the shape of the gain more than its size. Funds give owners a ready market for minority slices and staged sales. Portland's buyer took 80.1% at $4.0 billion with the remaining 19.9% priced at $4.5 billion by September 2028 ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/03/30/nba-approves-trail-blazers-sale-tom-dundon/), c; [amortization model](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/tax_mechanisms/nba_control_sales_sec197.csv)), and the Celtics' buyer took at least 51% at more than $6.1 billion with the rest due by 2028 at up to $7.3 billion ([AP via Western Mass News](https://www.westernmassnews.com/2025/08/14/nba-approves-sale-boston-celtics-private-equity-mogul-bill-chisholm-record-61-billion/), c). A clause triggered only by a control sale misses most of these transfers, and even Minnesota's Vikings law exempts capital infusions and transfers among existing owners of up to 20% ([Minn. Stat. 473J.15](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/473J.15), b), exceptions that could cover some fund investments, since every fund stake in Table 1 is 20% or less (a). No study found measures a private equity effect on NBA ticket prices, subsidy size or relocation threats. The federal price index for admission to sporting events rose 45.7% from 2010 to 2024 against 43.9% for all items (a, from [BLS](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SS62032), b), so ticket prices have not clearly outrun inflation on that measure, though it does not capture premium seating or fees. Critics such as economist Stefan Szymanski expect funds to try to "extract more of the financial value of that happiness from the fans" ([Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2025/03/19/san-francisco-giants-private-equity-pro-sports), c), while the league calls fund investment a "constructive way to support the league's continued growth" ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/the-nbas-expanding-private-equity-footprint/), c). The Ways and Means chairman summed up the subsidy pattern as "leverage, threaten, relocate, repeat" ([hearing summary](https://hlc.com/en/publications/ways-and-means-hearing-shows-continued-bipartisan-interest-in-removing-tax-breaks-for-sports-teams), c).

### What a gain-sharing clause would be worth now

For cities still negotiating, the most valuable tool found is a sale-or-transfer share modeled on Minnesota's Vikings law: 25% of the gain for ten years, 15% in years 11 to 15 and 10% in years 16 to 20 ([Minn. Stat. 473J.15](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/473J.15), b). Table 7 estimates its expected value (a) at growth rates well below the 18.7% a year observed since 2020, assuming a 4.6% annual chance of a control sale (nine NBA control changes in 6.5 years, from the [control-sale list](https://github.com/realvivek/NBA-Arena-Funding/blob/main/data/tax_mechanisms/nba_control_sales_sec197.csv), c), a 4% discount rate, a 30-year horizon and gains measured from the 2026 value (in Portland, from the $4.0 billion purchase). A city actually receives either nothing or a large payment: a Portland sale in year 10 at 8% growth would pay about $1.16 billion (a).

Equity would be worth more if values keep rising: a stake bought with the subsidy and sold at fair value in year 20 is worth 1.0, 2.1 or 4.4 times the subsidy in present value at 4%, 8% or 12% growth (a), but no NBA rule allowing government ownership of a team was found, and the public would carry the downside. The economists' review finds relocation threats have lost force, since leagues refill departed markets and several teams won public money without threats ([Bradbury, Coates and Humphreys retrospective](https://campus.kennesaw.edu/colleges-departments/coles/centers/markets-economic-opportunity/docs/bradbury-coates-humphreys-01-30-2023.pdf), b), which strengthens the hand of cities that ask for more than recent NBA deals have given.

## Conclusion

The arena deal is no longer the whole public bargain, and judging it alone understates what owners receive. In Portland the state, city and county put in $573 million (b), the buyer's write-off is worth an estimated $1.07 billion in federal tax over 15 years before any recapture (a), more federal cost rides on any tax-exempt bonds, and the public's claim on the team's appreciation is zero in every layer. As ownership shifts to financiers and funds whose business is buying and selling positions, the moments when value is realized multiply (fund entries and exits, staged buyouts, resales like the Lakers' within a year), and each sale can restart the federal write-off at a higher price while passing untouched through local agreements written for rare control sales. The league is not a neutral referee in that market: it shares in one fund's fees and has loosened its rules twice in under a year as large buyers needed room. Public negotiators therefore face counterparties whose return is the value public money helps create, and where a government's finance office did assess the question, in Washington, it found the publicly funded renovation would raise "long-term value for Monumental" and the tax break was "not financially necessary."

The practical consequence is that the public's protection has to be written before the next valuation step, not after it. A gain-share that catches minority, fund and staged transfers is the one realistic remedy whose expected value approaches the money cities are now committing, and Portland's December 31, 2026 documents, San Antonio's definitive agreements and Memphis's new lease will show whether any NBA city writes one. The records needed to test the rest of the story already exist and are mostly public: capital requisitions and vendor files at publicly owned arenas, naming agreements, consultant contracts, bond disclosures and pension commitment lists. Without that clause, the next sale of a team in a publicly rebuilt arena will pay the public what the last six did: nothing.
