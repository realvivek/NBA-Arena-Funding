# Who Paid for the NBA's Arenas

October 22, 2026

Taxpayers have put or committed $9.59B to today's 30 NBA arenas, 35% of the $27.46B spent on them (2026 dollars). Counting property-tax exemptions and taxes redirected to arena debt, public money reaches at least $11.56B, or 40%, and 47% of that came after the arenas opened. Another $3.3B is committed or pending for new and rebuilt arenas in Oklahoma City, Salt Lake City, San Antonio, Portland, Memphis and Houston.

The money has moved from building arenas to rebuilding them. Since 2014, public money to renovate or buy existing arenas has come to $3.02B, 1.5 times the $1.99B for new ones: $1.08B in the four arenas with public money that opened in that time and $910M for Oklahoma City's arena under construction.

Relocation threats worked as deadlines. Of 20 documented owner or league threats since 1994, 12 were followed by public money in the same city, 11 of them within two years, and Portland's money is approved but pending. Deals after a threat were no more lopsided than deals without one: at the median the public paid 64% after a threat and 78% without one.

Residents carried slightly more of it than visitors: their sales, property and general taxes supplied 43% of public capital, against 42% from hotel, car-rental and food taxes. The mix has turned: visitor taxes supplied 47% of the money dated before 2014, and residents' taxes have supplied 61% since. Almost none of the recent money went to voters, and the arenas of the league's 10 least valuable teams hold half of all of it. Most economic research finds that the money does not come back in local growth; economists still argue over whether that amounts to a consensus.

## All 30 arenas

Eighteen of the 30 arenas are publicly owned, and the public paid half or more of everything spent on 12 of them. Of the 12 owned privately or run privately on public land, 9 still got public money or a property-tax break, among them Chase Center (city transit capital) and Xfinity Mobile Arena (exempt as city-owned property, about $3.49M a year, not counted); we found none for Intuit Dome or Scotiabank Arena. TD Garden was approved in 1992 under a state urban-renewal law with negotiated tax payments of $1.0M to $1.5M a year, a break we could not put a number on.

## How the money was raised

Public arena money reaches teams through seven channels. The biggest run on taxes most voters never see, and several are built to keep a federal tax break or to stay out of the players' share of league revenue.

| Channel | How it works | NBA example | Latest verified figure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dedicated sales tax | A new local sales tax pledged to arena bonds | Jazz: Salt Lake City 0.5% tax ([Utah Tax Commission](https://tax.utah.gov/sales/distributions/)) | $57.99M collected in FY2026 |
| Visitor taxes | Hotel, car-rental and food taxes routed to an arena district | Bucks: Wisconsin Center District ([Legislative Fiscal Bureau](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/misc/lfb/informational_papers/january_2021/0018_local_professional_sports_facilities_financing_informational_paper_18.pdf)) | $49.97M in 2025, 62% from hotel rooms |
| Tax increment | Future property taxes captured for arena debt, school taxes included | Pistons: Little Caesars Arena ([Detroit DDA statements FY2024](https://www.degc.org/_files/ugd/69e7f0_b4d878cc4e8c4d5087abcfc6308e9d4f.pdf)) | $30.0M paid into the bond fund in FY2024 |
| Players' income tax | State income tax paid by players, visiting players included, sent to the team or an arena fund | Pelicans ([Louisiana auditor](https://lla.la.gov/publicreports.nsf/0/ae49cb44840fe57986258b0900722bdf/$file/0000454ba.pdf)); Blazers ([Oregon SB 1501](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2026R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/SB1501/Enrolled)) | $2.56M to the Pelicans in FY2023 |
| Operating payments | Annual public cash to the team for running a public building | Pacers ([Indianapolis CIB audit](https://www.in.gov/sboa/WebReports/83950A.pdf)) | $12.71M in 2023, plus $2M of Pacers loans forgiven |
| Tax-exempt bonds | A federal subsidy: interest is tax-free when general taxes, not arena revenue, repay the debt | Jazz: about $900M of Salt Lake City district bonds, 2025, up to $525M of it for the arena ([bond notice](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/files/1261183.pdf)) | About $7.9M a year in federal cost for the arena's share ($13.5M for the whole issue) (estimate, [Brookings method](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/gayerdrukkergold_stadiumsubsidies_090816.pdf)) |
| Land and property tax | $1 land leases, exemptions and assessment cuts | Bulls and Blackhawks: United Center appeal ([Cook County Board of Review data](https://datacatalog.cookcountyil.gov/resource/7pny-nedm.json)) | Main arena parcel cut from $29.3M to $12.6M assessed value in 2024 |

Five loopholes show up again and again:

- **Pledge taxes, not rent.** Federal law since 1986 denies tax-exempt status when arena revenue repays the debt, so cities pledge sales, hotel or property taxes instead. Golden 1 Center's $272.9M, repaid partly by the Kings, was sold as taxable debt.
- **Keep it out of the players' share.** The 2023 labor contract excludes construction money and relocation inducements from shared revenue, and lets New Orleans and Memphis keep player-tax payments outside it ([CBA, Art. VII §1(a)](https://imgix.cosmicjs.com/25da5eb0-15eb-11ee-b5b3-fbd321202bdf-Final-2023-NBA-Collective-Bargaining-Agreement-6-28-23.pdf)).
- **Let the beneficiary run the borrower.** Three of five trustees of Salt Lake City's arena bond district are executives of SEG, the Jazz owner's company. On June 17, 2025 two of them moved and seconded the district's reimbursement agreement with SEG, which passed unanimously ([minutes](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/files/1368579.pdf)).
- **Write off the purchase.** Buyers deduct most of a franchise's price over 15 years. The House voted in 2025 to halve that deduction; the final law dropped the cap, and MLB's lobbyist was the only one on record on it ([House lobbying filings](https://lobbyingdisclosure.house.gov/)).
- **Write in an upgrade clause.** Leases such as Houston's Toyota Center require the public owner to keep the building "first-class, state of the art," which turns aging arenas into new public bills.

## What taxpayers carry

The public money is concentrated: ten arenas hold 62% of it. The eight costliest buildings in 2026 dollars, from the Clippers' Intuit Dome to the Lakers' Crypto.com Arena, were built mostly with private money, though six of them got public money: $55M of transit capital for Chase Center, $139M for Crypto.com Arena and $234M to $455M each for Barclays Center, Little Caesars Arena, Golden 1 Center and American Airlines Center.

*Funding ledger compiled for this report from audits, bond documents and press. 30 current arenas, 2026 dollars (CPI-U). Data: [arena_ledger.csv](../data/ledger/arena_ledger.csv).*

The $9.59B leaves out public money that never shows up as a check. Federal taxpayers add an estimated $1.41B (present value at issue, in 2026 dollars) by exempting the interest on the bonds' new money for the arenas from tax, using Gayer, Drukker and Gold's published values for the seven issues in their data and a simpler estimate for later ones; $278M of it rests on five issues whose tax-exempt status is not confirmed. New York has excused Madison Square Garden from property tax since 1982, about $43M a year ([NYC Independent Budget Office](https://www.ibo.nyc.gov/assets/ibo/downloads/pdf/economy-and-employment/2023/2023-march-description-of-public-subsidies-for-madison-square-garden-yankee-stadium-citi-field-and-barclays-center.pdf)); the office estimates the city forwent more than $946.7M in fiscal 1984-2023, in 2023 dollars ([IBO, July 2023](https://www.ibo.nyc.gov/assets/ibo/downloads/pdf/taxes-and-other-revenues/2023/2023-july-an-examination-of-the-madison-square-garden-property-tax-exemption.pdf)). With the Department of Finance's values for fiscal 2024-2026 ([tax expenditure report](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/finance/downloads/pdf/reports/reports-tax-expenditure/ter_2026_final.pdf)), that is $1.16B in 2026 dollars.

DC's 2024 arena law continued Capital One Arena's tax exemptions to June 2050. Its own finance office valued that relief at $312.7M and found it "not financially necessary" ([DC CFO](https://cfo.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/ocfo/publication/attachments/TAFA_Capital%20One%20Arena%20112624.pdf)). The United Center's owners paid property tax under a 1989 state formula based on the arena's net income. We estimate it cut their taxes by $46.6M in 2002-2016 alone, $73M in 2026 dollars ([method](../data/property_tax/united_center_formula_savings.md)). Counting the Garden's exemption, that formula and the property tax redirected to Barclays Center's bonds lifts the public total to $11.56B.

The payments continue for decades after opening. Documented yearly payments to seven teams already total $753M. The largest are Miami-Dade County's and Florida's payments to the Heat's arena operator ([Inspector General](https://www.miamidadeig.org/resources-oig/pdf/Reports2012/IG11.34FinalBasketball.pdf), [OPPAGA](https://oppaga.fl.gov/Documents/Reports/15-01.pdf)) and Indianapolis's spending under its Pacers agreements ([CIB audit](https://www.in.gov/sboa/WebReports/83950A.pdf)). ## Who paid, who decided and who got it

Four patterns in the ledger did not show in totals alone. Each is computed from the committed data, and the queries are in `pipeline/stats.yaml`.

**Residents now pay most of it.** Items are dated as the ledger dates them: construction in the year an arena opened, renovations in the middle year of the work, purchases in the year of the sale and work under way in 2026. Of the $4.74B dated before 2014, visitor taxes supplied 47.2%, taxes residents mostly pay 27.2%, and land or money not split by stream 25.6%. Of the $4.10B dated since, residents' taxes supplied 61.4% and visitor taxes 35.9%, led by Salt Lake City's arena sales tax and Washington's general fund. Without those two deals, residents' share since 2014 is 47.8%, still well above the earlier 27.2%.

**Voters decided almost none of it.** Of the $4.10B since 2014, $148M (3.6%) came from taxes that voters approved: Oklahoma City's MAPS 4 sales tax, approved Dec. 10, 2019, which paid for $27M of Paycom Center improvements ([city](https://www.okc.gov/Infrastructure-Development/Current-Infrastructure-Projects/MAPS-History)), and Bexar County's venue tax, extended on May 10, 2008 for projects that included $75M of improvements to the arena now called Frost Bank Center ([election results](https://www.bexar.org/DocumentCenter/View/8963/May-10-2008-Media-Report-Report), [Bond Buyer](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/san-antonio-shopping-list)). City councils, county boards, legislatures and appointed boards approved the rest. A petition drive for a vote on Sacramento's arena failed to qualify for the ballot ([AP via Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/sports/sacramento-approves-477-million-arena-caps-off-struggle-to-build-new-home-for-nbas-kings.amp)), and Cleveland's referendum petitions were withdrawn in 2017 ([News 5](https://www.news5cleveland.com/sports/basketball/cavaliers/cavaliers-withdraw-from-140-million-quicken-loans-arena-transformation-project)). The two most recent votes were for new arenas outside the 30-arena total: Oklahoma City's in 2023 and Bexar County's in 2025.

**The least valuable teams got the most.** Ranked by Forbes's October 2026 values, the 10 least valuable teams play in arenas holding $4.77B of public money, 49.7% of it, and every one of those arenas took some. The middle 10 hold $3.41B. The 10 most valuable hold $1.41B, and five of their arenas took none. Counting the tax breaks for Madison Square Garden, Barclays Center and the United Center, the top 10 rise to $3.38B, still less. The rank correlation between a team's value and the public money in its arena is -0.60. A team's value tracks its market and the money its arena makes, so this shows where public money went, not why.

**Renovations now rival the buildings.** Four renovations with public money since 2014 cost more than their arenas did to build, in 2026 dollars: Indianapolis (2020-22), Phoenix (2019-20), Washington (under way) and Salt Lake City (under way). Salt Lake City's public money alone, $525M, is 2.3 times the Delta Center's 1991 cost. Construction prices have risen faster than consumer prices, so a construction cost index would shrink these multiples.

Miami's arena counts as privately built, but Miami-Dade County and Florida have paid its operator $335M since 1998 (2026 dollars), 4.5 times the county's land for the arena. The county's 2014 agreements schedule more: $6.4M a year through fiscal 2029, then $8.5M a year to June 2035, plus up to $2M a year of naming money through 2040 ([Commission Auditor](https://www.miamidade.gov/auditor//library/2014-06-03-bcc.pdf), [ACFR FY2025](https://www.miamidadeclerk.gov/resources-clerk/library/finance/cafr2025-complete.pdf)).

## What the deals promised, and what happened

Where a promise can be set against a record, housing and development promises fell short, revenue promises went both ways, and construction hiring goals were met in some cities and missed in others.

- **Sacramento.** The city's 2015 disclosure to investors in its $272.9M of Golden 1 Center bonds said parking and other arena-related revenue would carry the payments, and "in the six-year horizon ... no net effect on the General Fund is projected" ([remarketing circular, Appendix A](https://www.cityofsacramento.gov/content/dam/portal/treasurer/DebtManagement/officialstatements/lease-revenue-revenue/2015_Lease_Revenue_Bonds_Series_2015_(G1C)_Taxable.pdf), b). By 2023 the city had diverted "more than $5.7 million over the past two years" from its general fund to the bonds (Sacramento Bee, quoted by [Field of Schemes](https://www.fieldofschemes.com/2023/10/23/20519/why-the-kings-arena-parking-revenue-fiasco-is-bad-but-not-really-that-bad/), c). Its construction hiring went better: the city auditor found the project "appears to have exceeded its local hiring goals" ([follow-up audit, Dec. 5, 2017](http://sacramento.granicus.com/MetaViewer.php?view_id=21&event_id=3117&meta_id=508994), b).
- **Detroit.** "The project agreement requires at least half of the jobs created to be filled by Detroit residents" ([Gov. Snyder's office, July 24, 2013](https://www.michigan.gov/formergovernors/recent/snyder/press-releases/2013/07/24/detroit-arena-entertainment-project-expected-to-create-4380-construction-jobs-and-help-revitalize), b). Detroiters worked 25% of the nearly 3 million hours, and contractors paid $5.2M in fines ([Construction Dive, citing Crain's and a city report](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/local-hire-fines-for-detroit-arena-contractors-climb-to-52m/519688/), c). A 2026 peer-reviewed study found that sports events at the new arena "are not associated with a significant increase in demand" for hotel rooms and short-term rentals ([Jakar, Binesh and Kaplanidou, Journal of Sport Management](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsm.2025-0050), b).
- **Brooklyn.** The Atlantic Yards developer agreed in 2005 to reserve 20% of 4,500 rentals for low-income and 30% for middle-income households ([NYC Independent Budget Office](https://www.ibo.nyc.gov/assets/ibo/downloads/pdf/economy-and-employment/2005/2005-september-atlantic-yards-a-net-fiscal-benefit-for-the-city.pdf), b), 2,250 units. By the May 2025 deadline 1,374 were built, and the state "agreed to accept just $12 million in penalties for the 876 missing affordable housing units" ([City Limits, Sept. 24, 2026](https://citylimits.org/in-atlantic-yards-reboot-new-developers-seek-more-subsidies-2382-more-apartments-but-only-366-more-affordable-ones/), c), about seven months of the $2,000-a-month penalty per unit (a).
- **Miami.** The 1997 agreements gave the county 40% of the arena's net cash flow above $14M ([Inspector General](https://www.miamidadeig.org/resources-oig/pdf/Reports2012/IG11.34FinalBasketball.pdf), b). In 13 years the formula paid once, $257,134 in 2013, and the 2014 amendments deleted it ([Commission Auditor](https://www.miamidade.gov/auditor//library/2014-06-03-bcc.pdf), b).
- **Promises that held.** Washington's 1990s arena tax beat its forecast: "For each of the past 3 years, collections have exceeded the $9 million originally forecasted" ([GAO, 1998](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-98-223/pdf/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-98-223.pdf), b). Salt Lake City's council said its 0.5% sales tax would raise "around $1.2 billion over 30 years" ([council release, Oct. 1, 2024](https://www.slc.gov/council/salt-lake-city-council-approves-sports-entertainment-culture-and-convention-district/), b), $40M a year on average (a); it raised $58.0M in fiscal 2026, its first full year ([Utah State Tax Commission](https://tax.utah.gov/sales/distributions/), b).

## How these figures compare with other estimates

The most complete academic count of public money for sports venues, J.C. Bradbury's 2026 database ([data, CC BY 4.0](https://github.com/jcbradbury/This-One-Will-Be-Different/blob/260e4fcdb3f94e056fefaaf052b524bef99cbf73/data/venuesdb_base.csv)), takes Judith Grant Long's 2013 figures for arenas opened through 2010 and press reports after that. It puts public construction money in today's 30 arenas at $5.33B in 2026 dollars (c/a); this ledger has $5.39B before Barclays Center's PILOT bonds (a). The totals agree, but 17 of the 23 arenas with public money in either count differ by more than a tenth (a). Arenas where the ledger is higher add $928M; arenas where it is lower, $867M. The differences are mostly about scope: the ledger counts land, site work and transit at Washington, Los Angeles, Denver, Portland, Miami and Golden State, treats bonds Atlanta's team repaid as private, records Minneapolis's purchase separately, and keeps the PILOT bonds Bradbury partly counts for Barclays Center in its expanded total. Four splits remain unsettled in every source: Indianapolis, Atlanta, Memphis and New Orleans (29% to 100% public across compilations); their bond official statements on EMMA would settle them. Every gap is listed in `data/ledger/totals.txt`, section I.

Like most published estimates, the database counts only construction. The ledger adds $4.20B of purchases, renovations and yearly payments (a). Long's "full count" of the 99 major-league facilities in use in 2001 found that land, infrastructure, operations, municipal services and forgone property tax raised the average public subsidy by about 40% (c, [Journal of Sports Economics abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/1527002504264614)); the ledger's public money is already 1.78 times its construction money (a). Two of Long's categories are still missing here: property tax forgone at publicly owned arenas beyond the three breaks counted (Mark Propheter found 79% of 126 major-league venues fully exempt and $654.3M forgone in 2022, per [Field of Schemes's review](https://www.fieldofschemes.com/2024/02/07/20905/stadiums-and-arenas-are-set-to-collect-18b-in-property-tax-breaks-over-their-lifetimes/), c), and event-day city services. For the federal cost of tax-exempt bonds, the ledger now uses Gayer, Drukker and Gold's published present values for the seven NBA issues in their data ([Brookings data file](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/fulldata_gayerdrukkergold_federalsubsidiesstadiums_09082106.xlsx), c); its own simpler formula had run 13.8% above them.

## Relocation threats and what they bought

Threats worked as a deadline more than a discount. Of 20 documented owner or league threats since 1994, 12 were followed by public money in the same city: eight within seven months, four after more than a year. Portland's state money is approved but still pending. In four more, the team left and the new city paid.

Threat deals were not more lopsided. After a threat, the median deal was $308M in 2025 dollars and 64% public; without one, $247M and 78% public. Neither gap is statistically meaningful (p = 0.95 and 0.51).

The biggest recent deal needed no owner threat at all. Oklahoma City voters approved a sales tax for an arena of $900M or more in 2023, $850M of it public, 94% of the cost, after the mayor warned the team could leave ([PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/voters-in-oklahoma-city-will-decide-whether-a-new-thunder-arena-is-worth-the-investment)).

Cities that lost teams kept paying:

- **Oakland and Alameda County:** $55.7M of arena bonds were outstanding when the Warriors left in 2019. An arbitrator made the team liable for about $40M ([KTVU](https://www.ktvu.com/news/warriors-ordered-to-pay-40m-for-oracle-arena-upgrades)), and the Court of Appeal upheld it in 2020 ([A157688](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/A157688.PDF)).
- **Kansas City:** built an arena in 2007 to attract a team and still owes about $203M on it, with no NBA or NHL tenant ([KCUR](https://www.kcur.org/post/projects-kansas-city-taxpayers-are-still-paying)).
- **Newark:** pledged $210M toward Prudential Center, where the Nets played two seasons before Brooklyn.
- **Seattle:** the Sonics' $45M settlement paid off $34.2M of remaining KeyArena debt, so taxpayers were largely spared.

The money rarely comes back. A 2023 review of more than 130 studies found a "decades-old consensus of very limited economic impacts," with benefits "well short of covering public outlays" ([Bradbury, Coates and Humphreys](https://doi.org/10.1111/joes.12533)).
